### S21|Computer-based requests and tool roles|Unexpected file;Software request;Fake warning;Approved workflow|Inspect the requested action before opening or installing anything.|comparison
Computer-based social engineering can use websites, messages, pop-up warnings, shared documents, or other interfaces to influence an action. A fake support warning might ask someone to install remote-access software. A shared document might move a recipient toward an unexpected sign-in page. A prize message might ask for personal information. The technique depends on the story and requested behavior; an unfamiliar file is not automatically proven malware. Keep content inspection separate from execution and from evidence of a later system change. At Northstar, staff can route a software request through the managed application process and contact support using the known directory. Our exercise reads inert files and displays constructed interfaces without collecting input or starting services. Tools used for awareness simulations have legitimate governed roles, but their presence does not authorize a campaign. When documenting a tool, state its purpose, required approval, possible data collection, and the exact evidence its output can support.

### S22|Work an email decision step by step|Preserve original;Extract fields;Verify separately;Record decision|Preserve the artifact and verify the business action independently.|flow
Here is a complete email-review workflow for Northstar. Preserve the supplied message and record its hash before editing or forwarding it. Read the displayed identity, full addresses, reply path, subject, body, link targets, and any stipulated receiver results. Write the exact requested business action in one sentence. Compare the request with the established supplier or service record. If verification is required, obtain the contact from that independent record rather than the message. Record who verified the request, through which channel, with what authority, and whether the action was approved or stopped. Our offline verifier checks the synthetic email fields and the associated fixture records. It does not send mail, resolve links, or authenticate a real domain. A real organization would also follow its approved reporting and evidence-handling process. The goal is a reviewable decision: what was requested, what was actually verified, and what additional evidence would be needed to claim a completed harmful action.

### S23|Vishing and the callback boundary|Caller claim;Displayed number;Known directory;Verified callback|A convincing voice and caller ID remain claims to verify.|flow
Vishing uses voice communication to support a deceptive request. A caller can claim to be an employee, supplier, support technician, or manager. Caller ID may be misleading, and a familiar-sounding voice is not reliable proof of identity or authority. Synthetic media can add realism, but ordinary persuasion and account knowledge can also make a call convincing. Do not assume that every convincing suspicious call was generated by AI. At Northstar, a caller asks Eli to reset a staff authenticator and provides a number for verification. Calling that supplied number returns to the same untrusted source. Eli instead follows the established recovery policy and uses independently maintained contact or identity evidence. A callback is useful only when the number and the procedure are appropriate for the action. For the lesson, a written fictional call transcript carries the request. We do not clone a real person's voice, call a participant, or treat vocal resemblance as a security control.

### S24|Smishing and mobile context|Short message;Small display;Unexpected link;Known application|A message preview is not enough to verify a sensitive request.|comparison
Smishing uses text messages to carry a phishing request. Mobile devices can make the decision harder because a preview hides details, a shortened address obscures the destination, or a user is moving between tasks. Similar requests can arrive through messaging applications. A message claiming that a class account is expiring might ask the recipient to follow a link immediately. The practical question is whether the account change exists in the known service and whether the requested action belongs to its normal process. At Northstar, learners can open the established portal directly and contact support through its published route. They do not need to reply to the suspicious sender to prove that the request is false. Preserve the message according to the organization's reporting process while minimizing unnecessary personal-data copying. A local worksheet can compare the message, the approved announcement record, and the actual requested action without sending a text or opening a live destination.

### S25|QR codes hide a destination|Encoded text;Destination context;Requested action;Known portal|A QR code encodes data; it does not certify the destination.|layers
A QR code is a way to encode information, often a URL. Its visual pattern does not prove who placed it, who controls the destination, or what action is authorized there. A code on a familiar-looking notice can redirect a learner toward a different site or an unexpected sign-in request. Not every QR code is suspicious, and a URL inspection alone cannot establish the safety of the complete workflow. At Northstar, compare the notice with the approved learning announcement and open the known portal independently when a sensitive action is requested. The teaching fixture uses a reserved test-domain address and contains no functioning login service. An offline decoder can report the encoded string; that is evidence about the image bytes, not a live website assessment. In your report, separate the encoded destination, the notice's claimed owner, the requested action, and any actual interaction record. Never turn an unmeasured scan into a claim that credentials were submitted or stolen.

### S26|Social platforms and borrowed trust|Public profile;Known account;Shared contact;Requested permission|A familiar account can still carry an unauthorized request.|comparison
Social platforms provide context such as names, interests, employment claims, and shared contacts. That context can make an approach feel personal. An account may be fabricated, impersonated, or compromised, while a genuine person can still make a request outside their authority. A connection badge or a long conversation history is therefore not a substitute for a sensitive-action check. Suppose a Northstar staff account asks a colleague to move a supplier discussion into a private chat and approve a changed payment instruction. The colleague should use the finance process rather than treating the platform relationship as approval. Assess only information and interactions within the authorized scope. Our lesson uses fictional profiles and message records, not real-profile harvesting. Keep the evidence narrow: a supplied message establishes its content, an account record may establish activity, and attribution to a particular person requires more support. The useful defense links a known business process to an independent verification path.

### S27|A second channel can share the same source|Email request;Supplied number;Follow-up text;Independent record|Two messages are not independent proof when one source controls both.|tree
An attacker can reinforce one channel with another. An email is followed by a call, and the caller sends a text containing the same urgent instruction. Repetition can make the story seem confirmed even when the same source controls every contact. Independence comes from the trusted record and procedure, not from counting channels. At Northstar, a payment-change request supplies a callback number and a messaging address. Both remain part of the request until verified elsewhere. June should obtain the supplier contact through the approved directory or an established record and apply the required approval policy. A compromised trusted contact path may require escalation rather than repeated use of the same evidence. Document where each verification fact came from. This source map is especially helpful when reviewing a chain of forwarded messages. It prevents a copied claim from being counted as several confirmations and makes the next independent check clear to the responsible owner.

### S28|Identity theft and account control|Personal data;Identity claim;Account access;Business action|Possessing identity details is different from proving the right to act.|layers
Identity theft uses another person's identifying information without authorization for a harmful purpose. Account takeover concerns unauthorized control of an account. The two can connect, but neither term should replace the evidence needed for the other. Knowing an employee's name, date of employment, or project history does not prove that the caller is that employee. A successful authentication event does not establish that every later action was authorized by its owner. At Northstar, protect personal information according to its purpose and retention rules, and avoid using easily discovered facts as the only recovery check. If a record shows a request for recovery, look for the actual identity-verification result, authenticator change, notifications, and subsequent account activity before describing an outcome. The fictional fixtures intentionally leave some of those states unknown. A careful response can protect the account while preserving uncertainty about who made the request and whether any unauthorized access occurred.

### S29|Insider risk requires careful attribution|Legitimate access;Policy boundary;Observed action;Intent unknown|A concerning event does not by itself prove malicious intent.|comparison
An insider risk involves someone with a trusted relationship or legitimate access creating a security concern. Some situations involve deliberate misuse. Others involve mistakes, coercion, compromised accounts, or unclear processes. Access to a system is not evidence of malicious intent, and an alert should not become an unsupported accusation against a person. Start with the event, the relevant policy, the business context, and the quality of attribution. At Northstar, a staff account might request an unusual export or approve a change outside its normal role. Determine what the account actually did and whether the action was permitted before making a person-level claim. Preserve evidence through the authorized security, privacy, and personnel process. Apply least privilege, separation of duties, reviewable approvals, and accessible reporting. In the lesson, we analyze synthetic records and propose an investigation question. We do not label a fictional staff member malicious merely because their name appears in a suspicious thread.

### S30|MFA fatigue and approval requests|Unexpected prompt;Repeated requests;User report;Account review|Repeated prompts need investigation, not an approval to make them stop.|timeline
Multi-factor authentication reduces many account risks, but its design and use matter. MFA fatigue pressures a person to approve an unexpected authentication request, sometimes through repeated prompts and a support pretext. A caller may also ask for a one-time code. An unexpected prompt should lead to the established deny, report, and account-review process, not approval simply to stop the interruptions. The presence of a prompt does not by itself prove that an attacker successfully authenticated or obtained the password through phishing. Review the relevant identity-service records and the authentication method. Phishing-resistant authentication binds the protocol to the legitimate verifier in a way that ordinary transferable codes do not. It still does not authorize every business action or repair an insecure recovery process. At Northstar, Eli distinguishes a reported prompt, an approved challenge, an authenticator reset, and a later session. Each requires separate evidence and may call for a different response.

### S31|Account recovery is a security boundary|Recovery request;Identity proof;Authorized reset;Notification|A strong sign-in method depends on a governed recovery path.|flow
Recovery exists because legitimate users lose devices, forget secrets, or become unable to authenticate. A process that restores access must protect against impostors while remaining workable for those users. The help desk should follow approved identity evidence, role permissions, escalation, logging, and notification rules. A convincing story or knowledge of public details is not an equivalent replacement. At Northstar, Eli receives a request to replace an authenticator before a class begins. The ticket records the request, but no reset event is supplied. The correct conclusion is that recovery was requested and its completion remains unmeasured. A useful verification plan checks the identity-service change record, the authorized approval, notification delivery, and subsequent session activity where appropriate. Do not weaken the recovery process merely because the front-door sign-in uses a strong authenticator. For teaching, the complete recovery chain is shown as a decision diagram with explicit states, rather than animating an account takeover as an observed fact.

### S32|Threat-model the recovery and approval paths|Entry path;Fallback path;Owner;Verification test|Protect the alternate route as well as the normal route.|tree
A threat model follows the routes by which a protected state can change. For an account, those routes include ordinary sign-in, recovery, device enrollment, administrator changes, and support escalation. For finance, they include supplier creation, bank-detail changes, payment approval, and emergency exceptions. Attackers may prefer the route with the weakest verification rather than attacking the strongest control directly. At Northstar, draw the normal path and each authorized exception. Name the owner, required evidence, logs, and notifications for every branch. An exception can be legitimate, but it still needs a defined authority and a reviewable result. Test both sides of the intended behavior: a legitimate user or supplier should complete the approved process, while an unverified request should be held or escalated. Our capstone asks you to propose these checks. A diagram or policy statement is supporting material; only actual authorized execution can establish that a control worked in its target environment.

### S33|Ask one measurable assessment question|Business question;Allowed population;Defined outcome;Protection plan|Design the assessment around a decision the owner can improve.|flow
Begin an awareness assessment with a question that the responsible owner can act on. For example, can finance staff find the independent supplier-verification route, or can employees report an unexpected recovery request? Avoid a vague objective such as proving that people are vulnerable. Define the participant population, channel, task, time window, permitted observations, and outcome before choosing a tool. Specify how legitimate work and personal information will be protected. An assessment can examine a written scenario, a training interface, or another approved format; deceptive live delivery is not required for every learning goal. Northstar's exercise is a no-send review of constructed evidence. It measures whether the learner can identify the requested action, verification source, known facts, and next check in a worksheet. That is a conceptual assessment outcome, not proof of behavior during an actual campaign. Keep the measurement aligned with the question and explain the practical decision it is intended to support.

### S34|Document governance before tooling|Sponsor;Scope owner;Stop contact;Debrief|The tool operates inside the approved assessment, not outside it.|checklist
An assessment plan should identify a sponsor, a scope owner, the operational contact, and the person with authority to stop the activity. State which populations, channels, themes, dates, and observations are allowed. Document exclusions, incident escalation, data minimization, retention, and the debrief approach. Resolve the applicable participant-protection and consent requirements with the appropriate organizational owners. A signed tool purchase or a general security role does not substitute for this specific plan. At Northstar, the no-send plan includes fictional recipients and inert artifacts only. A stop condition could be unexpected personal data in an input, an artifact resolving to an unintended destination, or a request to involve a real person. The response is to preserve the issue and return to the scope owner. A useful plan also tells support staff how to handle reports constructively. Otherwise, a simulation can measure confusion created by its own process rather than the security capability the organization wanted to understand.

### S35|Know what simulation tools actually do|Template design;Delivery options;Interaction records;Governance|Tool output supports only the event and environment actually exercised.|comparison
Phishing simulation tools can help organize templates, recipient groups, delivery settings, landing pages, and interaction reports. GoPhish is one example of an awareness-simulation platform. The Social-Engineer Toolkit includes broader assessment functions whose activation requires careful scope and authorization. We discuss these tool roles to understand the workflow and evidence surfaces; this lesson does not launch campaigns or credential-collection services. Tool installation would prove only installation. A campaign configuration would prove only configuration. A dashboard count requires an explanation of how the event was recorded, which population it covers, and what possible automated activity or missing telemetry affects it. At Northstar, native diagrams show the relationship between an approved plan, inert training material, and a review record. Supplied synthetic interaction rows are clearly labeled as fictional. The tool does not decide consent, legitimate business authority, participant welfare, or whether a reported interaction demonstrates compromise. Those remain responsibilities of the assessment design and evidence review.

### S36|Choose fair outcome measures|Eligible population;Known exposure;Defined event;Detection difficulty|A rate needs a defined numerator, denominator and task context.|layers
A click rate is a ratio, not a complete explanation. Define the eligible population, attempted delivery, confirmed delivery where available, event definition, and observation period. A failed delivery should not silently count as a person resisting a message. An automated link scanner may create an interaction that is not a human decision. Reporting rate and time-to-report can answer different operational questions, but they also require clear definitions and appropriate data handling. The NIST Phish Scale helps describe the difficulty of detecting an email based on observable cues and the relationship between its premise and the recipient's context. It is not a universal score of a person's security ability. At Northstar, compare scenarios only after documenting their context and measurement rules. Our fixture calculations describe a constructed table with known rows. They do not establish real employee performance or a statistically valid population result. Use the evidence to select a practical process improvement and a suitable later validation.

### S37|Debrief and improve the workflow|Explain the cue;Recognize reporting;Repair friction;Plan recheck|Learning and process repair are more useful than a blame ranking.|flow
A constructive debrief explains the scenario, the requested action, the relevant warning signs, and the approved verification route. It should also address the context that made the task difficult. A person who reported a message or asked for help may have provided valuable defensive evidence even if another interaction occurred. Avoid treating an ambiguous click record as a character judgment. At Northstar, finance staff explain whether the supplier directory was easy to find, and help-desk staff explain whether the recovery escalation path was clear. The assessment owner can improve those processes and define the next test. Protect individual information through the approved retention and reporting policy. Aggregate measures should preserve meaningful denominators and relevant scenario differences. Our student worksheet asks for a specific improvement, owner, and revalidation check. Completing that worksheet demonstrates the learner's recorded reasoning only; a proposed change does not become an accepted operational control until its actual target behavior is tested.

### S38|Receive and preserve a report|Original artifact;Reporter context;Time reference;Evidence location|Make reporting easy and preserve the original evidence safely.|flow
Detection often begins when someone reports an unexpected request. A good reporting route is easy to find and gives the person a clear next step. The response team should preserve the original artifact, the reported time and context, and the location of relevant system records through its approved process. Screenshots can be useful, but they may omit addresses, headers, link targets, or sequence details. Forwarding can also change the evidence context. At Northstar, the supplied email file and ticket record remain separate from the learner's interpretation. Record hashes for the local fixtures and retain the parser command and result. Minimize copying of unnecessary personal information, and do not open a suspicious destination simply to make the report more complete. A report establishes that a concern was raised. Stronger claims about delivery, interaction, authentication, money movement, or physical entry need their own evidence. This supports quick protective action without forcing premature certainty about the full incident.

### S39|Triage by action and consequence|Requested change;Known interaction;Protected resource;Response owner|Prioritize the observed risk and route it to the responsible owner.|comparison
Triage asks what action was requested, what interaction is known, what resource is at risk, and who can take the next protective step. An unusual invoice request belongs with finance verification as well as security review. A reported code disclosure may require identity-service response. A suspicious visitor request belongs with the established physical-security process. The absence of malware does not remove a business-fraud risk, and a frightening message does not establish a completed compromise. At Northstar, use the evidence ledger to record the strongest supported state and the immediate question that changes the response. If a payment is merely requested, verify and hold it under the applicable process. If a real payment were confirmed, the authorized finance and incident-response owners would follow their established escalation. Our fixtures do not supply such a transaction. State urgency from observed consequences and organizational policy, rather than inventing impact to justify a dramatic narrative.

### S40|Correlate records without inventing identity|Mail record;Ticket record;Identity event;Business record|Correlated events need reliable keys, timing and attribution limits.|timeline
Different records describe different surfaces. A mail record may show delivery. A help-desk ticket may show a requested change. An identity-service record may show an authenticator event. A finance record may show approval or movement of funds. Correlate them using reliable identifiers, time references, and documented relationships. Similar names or nearby timestamps can suggest a lead without proving that the same person caused every event. Account compromise can further separate an account identity from the human using it. At Northstar, the fixture manifest supplies explicit case identifiers and fictional time context so that the relationships are inspectable. Those relationships are constructed teaching assumptions, not findings from a live campus. The offline verifier measures the actual files and checks their declared links. In your brief, distinguish the correlation rule, the observations it joins, and the remaining attribution uncertainty. A useful next check is one that can separate plausible explanations rather than merely repeat the same source in another format.
