### S01|The Northstar trust mission|Request;Identity;Authority;Evidence|A believable request still needs a verified decision path.|flow
Welcome to Module Nine, Social Engineering. Tally returns to Northstar Learning Center to investigate a different kind of security question. A message asks the finance team to change a payment destination. A caller asks the help desk to recover an account. A visitor asks an employee to hold a secured door. Each request can look ordinary, and each can move information, money, or access across a boundary. Our task is to explain the mechanism, identify the decision that matters, and choose evidence that supports a response. Earlier modules examined services, weaknesses, malware behavior, and packets. Those technical observations do not automatically establish who authorized a human request. We will connect people, processes, and technology throughout one fictional campus. You will learn common techniques, assessment methods, detection, response, and practical controls. Every Northstar message and event in this lesson is synthetic teaching material. The exercises analyze supplied records without sending messages or involving real participants.

### S02|Break the trust chain into checks|Who asks?;Which action?;Whose authority?;Which evidence?|Identity, permission and the requested action are separate checks.|flow
Start from the resource being protected. It might be a staff account, a student record, a payment instruction, or access to an office. Next identify the exact requested action. Reading an announcement differs from entering a password, approving a payment, or resetting an authenticator. Then ask how the requester's identity is established and whether that identity has authority for this action. A familiar name is a claim. A successful login can establish control of an account, but the account may be compromised or lack the required permission. Finally, inspect the evidence and the verification channel. A phone number supplied inside the suspicious message belongs to the same trust problem as the message itself. A useful decision record separates requester, action, authority, verification path, and result. At Northstar, we will reuse those fields in every case. This method helps us find the specific broken check instead of calling every uncomfortable interaction a proven attack.

### S03|One campus and four workflows|Finance;Help desk;Learning portal;Office entrance|Keep the same campus while following different approval paths.|comparison
Northstar keeps its existing campus systems and device identities. STAFF remains an employee endpoint, SMTP handles mail transfer, and DIR provides the training directory role. Those network labels are not people and do not by themselves approve business actions. For this module we add fictional workflow roles without changing the inherited network map. June coordinates finance approvals. Eli works at the help desk. Mara coordinates office visitors. The learning team maintains classroom announcements. A supplier payment change should follow the finance process. Account recovery should follow the identity and authenticator process. A notice should lead learners through the known portal. A visitor should match an approved appointment and access policy. These workflows share a need for independent verification, but their owners and evidence differ. Our four cases will return to these same roles and records. Keep the distinction between a role authorized by policy and a name merely printed on a message.

### S04|Define an authorized assessment|Written scope;Participant care;Stop rules;Data handling|A useful assessment has an owner, scope and stopping authority.|checklist
A social engineering assessment evaluates a defined security question under explicit organizational authority. Approval should identify the sponsor, relevant owners, participant population, allowed channels, dates, data handling, support contacts, and stop conditions. Organizational approval alone does not settle every privacy or participant-protection requirement. The assessment owner must resolve those requirements before execution. A harmless-looking test can still cause distress, expose personal data, disrupt work, or trigger an incident response. Decide how reports will be handled and how participants will receive a constructive explanation. Our Northstar package uses a no-send assessment: you inspect supplied fictional artifacts and propose a governed test plan. It does not authorize sending deceptive messages, making calls, entering premises, or collecting credentials. That bounded exercise still develops valuable skills. You can define a measurable question, inspect the trust chain, select protective controls, and explain what a future authorized test would need to prove.

### S05|Separate observation from outcome|Supplied record;Interpretation;Unknown state;Next check|A suspicious request does not establish a completed compromise.|layers
Evidence has layers. A supplied email file can establish the bytes in that file. A trusted mail-service record may support a delivery claim. A report that someone clicked a link does not establish that a password was submitted. A password submission does not establish a successful login, and a successful login does not establish a completed payment. Each stronger statement needs its own evidence. The same discipline applies to calls and physical access. A ticket may record a recovery request without showing a reset. A door event may record an opening without identifying everyone who passed through. In this lesson, invented campus observations carry the label simulated output. Actual offline parser results describe the constructed files, not a real campus incident. Use four columns in your notes: observed, interpreted, unknown, and next check. This makes a useful report possible even when the strongest conclusion is that further verification is needed.

### S06|Pressure changes the decision|Urgency;Workload;Consequences;Safe pause|Urgency is a cue to verify the action through the right process.|comparison
An urgent request can be legitimate. A class may really be starting, a supplier may really have a deadline, and an employee may really be locked out. Social engineering uses those ordinary pressures to shorten the decision process or redirect it toward an untrusted channel. The mechanism is not a magical ability to control another person's mind. It is a situation in which a requested action receives less verification than its consequences require. Ask what decision the pressure is trying to accelerate. Is it changing bank details, sharing a code, installing software, or allowing entry? At Northstar, the finance request says a payment must leave before a meeting. June can acknowledge the deadline while using the established verification and approval route. A safe pause should lead to an actionable alternative, such as a verified callback or escalation owner. Telling people simply to be more careful does not repair a workflow that makes verification impractical.

### S07|Authority and social proof|Claimed leader;Official appearance;Other people;Independent proof|Appearance and popularity do not establish authorization.|comparison
A message can borrow authority from a job title, a familiar organization, a uniform, or an official-looking document. Social proof adds a different cue: other people supposedly completed the action, so the recipient is encouraged to follow. Scarcity can make the opportunity or access seem about to disappear. These cues may appear together, and naming one does not prove that the message is fraudulent. The important question is whether they are replacing a required check. Suppose a Northstar notice claims that the director ordered all staff to complete a new account verification immediately. The director's name and a copied campus logo establish only the appearance presented by the notice. Staff can open the known internal announcement channel and confirm the request with the responsible owner. An authentic request should survive that check. An assessment should record the cue, the requested action, the verification route, and the result rather than awarding certainty from a logo alone.

### S08|Reciprocity and small commitments|Helpful favor;Shared interest;Small agreement;Larger request|A prior favor does not grant permission for a sensitive action.|flow
Reciprocity is the tendency to respond to a favor with cooperation. Liking and shared interests can make an interaction feel comfortable. Commitment can make a later request seem like a natural continuation of an earlier small agreement. These are ordinary human tendencies, not evidence of wrongdoing. A deceptive pretext can exploit them by moving from harmless conversation toward information or access that should require a separate decision. For example, a visitor helps carry supplies and then asks Mara to bypass the visitor desk. Helping with supplies does not establish an approved appointment or access permission. A caller who correctly mentions a staff project still needs to complete the authorized recovery process. The practical defense is to make the sensitive boundary explicit. People can remain courteous while routing the request through the right owner. In your analysis, identify the moment when an ordinary social interaction becomes a request to change a protected state.

### S09|Follow the request lifecycle|Context;Relationship;Requested action;Result evidence|Analyze the transition from a story to a protected action.|timeline
A useful social engineering lifecycle begins with context gathering, moves through contact and a believable story, reaches a requested action, and ends with an outcome or an unresolved attempt. Different sources divide these stages differently. The purpose of the model is to locate evidence and intervention points, not to force every event into one rigid sequence. Public information can make a pretext more specific, but knowing a name, schedule, or supplier does not prove authority. A relationship can be fabricated, borrowed from a compromised account, or entirely genuine while the requested action is still inappropriate. At Northstar, map each case to the first sensitive action: changing a payment record, resetting an authenticator, using an unexpected portal, or allowing entry. Record which stage is actually supported by the supplied evidence. A prevention control may stop the action, while a reporting control may improve detection and response after contact. Measure those effects separately.

### S10|Design a process people can use|Clear route;Accessible support;Workable timing;Constructive review|Controls work better when verification fits the real task.|checklist
A security process should make the correct next action understandable and available. If employees must choose between finishing urgent work and finding an unknown security contact, the organization has created a design problem. Useful controls provide a known callback directory, clear approval ownership, reliable recovery support, and an easy reporting route. They also consider accessibility, language, shift coverage, and the cost of false alarms. At Northstar, June should know who can approve a payment change when the usual manager is absent. Eli should have a recovery escalation path that does not depend on guessing whether a caller sounds convincing. Mara should be able to welcome a visitor without personally confronting a possible intruder. Review incidents and simulations to improve these processes. Describe the failed check and the conditions around it. A constructive debrief can recognize reporting and careful verification while explaining how the organization will remove the friction that made the unsafe shortcut attractive.

### S11|Impersonation creates a claim|Borrowed name;Plausible role;Sensitive request;Role verification|A role must be verified before its permissions are used.|flow
Impersonation presents an identity or role that the requester is not entitled to use. A pretext is the surrounding story that makes the contact and request seem reasonable. A caller may claim to be an employee, a support technician, a supplier, or a senior manager. The story can contain correct details while the claimed role remains unverified. Consider a Northstar visitor who says a technician is expected and points to a printed work order. That document may be genuine, altered, outdated, or simply unrelated to the person holding it. Mara needs the visitor process: confirm the appointment through the established owner, verify the required identity evidence, and apply the approved escort and access rules. The document's appearance is one observation within that process. For analysis, write down the claimed identity, claimed authority, requested access, and independent record needed. This separates impersonation risk from assumptions about a person's clothing, confidence, or manner.

### S12|Elicitation and information boundaries|Ordinary question;Combined details;Need to know;Approved disclosure|Several small disclosures can support a convincing later request.|layers
Elicitation obtains information through conversation rather than a direct demand for a secret. An apparently ordinary question about a work schedule, software product, reporting line, or current project can reveal useful context. Each detail may appear low risk by itself. Combined details can make a later impersonation more credible or expose a recovery route. Do not respond by assuming that every curious person is hostile. Define what information is public, what requires a business purpose, and who may approve disclosure. At Northstar, public class times can be discussed, while private staff recovery details and unpublished visitor schedules follow a different policy. A staff member can offer the public contact page or route a specialized request to its owner. In a no-send assessment, inspect fictional conversations and identify the first disclosure that crosses the stated boundary. Explain the potential use of that information without claiming that later exploitation occurred or identifying a real person as malicious.

### S13|Bait and promised benefits|Interesting object;Offered help;Requested trade;Sensitive action|Judge the required action separately from the promised benefit.|comparison
Baiting offers something attractive to encourage an action, such as opening a file, using an unknown device, or visiting an unexpected site. Quid pro quo describes a promised exchange, such as support or a reward in return for information or cooperation. The categories can overlap with phishing or impersonation. Classify the mechanism that matters instead of treating the labels as mutually exclusive boxes. A Northstar message might promise a classroom resource if staff install an unexpected application. The useful question is whether that installation comes through the authorized software process. An unknown USB device raises a device-handling question even when no malware has been demonstrated. Our lesson uses drawings and inert records; it does not connect unknown devices or execute content. A strong explanation names the promised benefit, the requested action, the protected resource, and the appropriate verification path. It also preserves uncertainty about what the file or device would actually do.

### S14|Tailgating and physical access|Secured door;Authorized person;Following visitor;Access decision|An open door does not establish permission for every entrant.|flow
Tailgating commonly refers to gaining entry by following someone through an access-controlled boundary. Some training materials use piggybacking to distinguish entry with a person's awareness or assistance; terminology varies, so state the behavior when precision matters. A person holding a door may be acting courteously without knowing whether the next entrant is authorized. Physical controls should support a safe alternative, such as directing visitors to reception or contacting an escort. Staff should follow the organization's safety and escalation process rather than confronting an unknown person. At Northstar, a badge event records that a credential was presented and a door state changed. It does not necessarily identify every person who entered or establish the intent behind the event. Compare the event with the visitor appointment, escort record, and other authorized evidence. A planned awareness test should use approved participants and physical-safety rules; our package analyzes a fictional door record only.

### S15|Protect visible and discarded data|Screen view;Printed page;Shared space;Secure disposal|Information exposure depends on what can actually be observed.|comparison
Shoulder surfing observes information as another person reads or enters it. It can involve a screen, keyboard, badge, printed document, or another visible surface. Dumpster diving seeks information in discarded material. Neither label proves that usable credentials or confidential records were obtained. Begin with the exposed information and the observation conditions. A photograph of an office desk may show a document without making its contents readable. A discarded page may be public material rather than protected data. At Northstar, apply screen positioning, appropriate privacy measures, clean handling of sensitive documents, and approved disposal according to the information's classification. Security should fit the work rather than requiring every ordinary page to be treated as a secret. In the fictional exercise, identify which record could reveal a recovery detail or a private schedule, then propose a control and a verification check. Do not collect real personal information to make the lesson feel realistic.

### S16|Use human and technical checks together|Reception process;Role directory;Approved device;Reporting route|One control supports a decision; no single cue proves the whole story.|layers
Human-facing requests often cross technical and organizational boundaries at the same time. A support caller can ask for remote access. A visitor can ask to use a workstation. A friendly contact can ask for an internal document. The correct response depends on the requested action and the established process, not on whether the interaction seems pleasant. At Northstar, combine a role directory with independent contact, approved device management, visitor handling, and clear escalation. Technical telemetry can show that software was installed or a session began. It cannot by itself establish that the person requesting it had valid business authority. Likewise, a manager's reported approval needs a reliable record when a sensitive action requires formal authorization. Build an evidence chain across the two surfaces. State what the people process verified, what the technical record observed, and what remains unknown. This prepares us to analyze email, mobile, and account-recovery cases with the same disciplined questions.

### S17|Phishing follows an action chain|Message;Interaction;Data entry;Account use|Delivery, clicking, submission and account use are different outcomes.|timeline
Phishing uses a deceptive message or interaction to induce an unsafe action or disclosure. Email is a common channel, but the mechanism also appears through messaging, voice, social platforms, and QR codes. The request may seek credentials, a payment, a file opening, software installation, or another change in behavior. Track the action chain carefully. A delivered message is not evidence that it was read. A link interaction is not evidence that credentials were entered. A submitted secret does not show that an attacker successfully used it. Additional controls may interrupt later steps. Northstar's fictional portal notice illustrates these separate stages. Our supplied files contain a notice and related context, not a real credential collection service. A good assessment defines the specific outcome being measured before it starts. A good response preserves evidence at the observed stage and checks the next relevant systems without claiming a complete compromise from a single suspicious message.

### S18|Compare targeting and business fraud|Broad phishing;Spear phishing;Whaling;BEC|Targeting describes the audience; BEC describes a business fraud pattern.|comparison
Broad phishing sends similar deceptive requests to many recipients. Spear phishing tailors the contact to a person or group. Whaling usually refers to targeting high-value or senior individuals. Business email compromise, often called BEC, concerns deceptive business communications that seek a harmful business action, frequently involving payments or sensitive information. These terms describe different dimensions and can overlap. A carefully targeted supplier-payment message can be both spear phishing and part of a BEC attempt. BEC does not require a malware attachment. It may use a compromised legitimate mailbox, an impersonated address, or another misleading communication path. At Northstar, focus on the bank-detail change rather than treating the absence of an attachment as safety evidence. Check the supplier record, independent contact, approval workflow, and actual payment status. The labels help organize the case, while the mechanism and evidence determine the control. Avoid assuming that every executive-themed email is whaling if the actual target is a finance employee.

### S19|Read the complete message context|Displayed name;Actual address;Reply path;Requested action|A familiar display name is only one field in the evidence.|layers
An email interface can emphasize a display name while hiding the full address. The From field, Reply-To field, envelope sender, links, attachments, and conversation context may point in different directions. Some differences are legitimate, such as a mailing service used by an organization. Others deserve investigation. Read the complete fields and compare them with a known record before drawing a conclusion. A visible link label may differ from its underlying destination, and a reply can be routed somewhere other than the apparent author. At Northstar, the offline parser exposes these fields from a synthetic message without opening links or attachments. Its output proves which values are encoded in the fixture. It does not perform live domain ownership checks or establish who controlled an account at the time. Record the requested action alongside the fields. A technically ordinary message can still ask for an unauthorized payment change, while a strange-looking delivery detail may have an approved explanation.

### S20|Understand mail authentication limits|SPF;DKIM;DMARC;Business authority|Domain authentication does not approve a payment or prove benign intent.|layers
SPF evaluates whether a sending source is authorized for a domain used in the mail transaction. DKIM validates a signature associated with a signing domain and the signed message content. DMARC uses alignment between the visible author domain and a qualifying SPF or DKIM result, together with a domain policy. These mechanisms address particular forms of domain impersonation. They do not prove that the human requester is honest or authorized to change a business record. A compromised legitimate mailbox may send an authenticated malicious request. A lookalike domain can authenticate its own mail successfully. Authentication-Results fields also require a trusted receiving context; arbitrary text added by a sender is not an independent measurement. Northstar's synthetic message supplies stipulated receiver results for discussion. The local parser reads those strings but does not execute SPF, DKIM, or DMARC verification. Keep that boundary explicit, and use the established finance approval process even when a trusted receiver reports an authentication pass.
