# M09 — Social Engineering — instructor key v1.0.0

[Question form](m09.md) · [Administration and version rules](README.md)

Use only after an answer or drill batch is committed. Explanations are in plain English. Options describe different mechanisms or decisions; use the facts in the stem to select the best fit. Original scores remain unchanged after this teaching. For an error, add at least three distinct retests within the endpoint.

### CEH26-M09-Q001

**Answer: D — Phishing**

Phishing uses deceptive messages to induce harmful actions or disclosure, commonly through email or other electronic messaging.

Why the other choices do not identify the asked-for mechanism or decision:

- **A — Spear phishing:** Spear phishing tailors deception to a particular person or group using relevant context.
- **B — Vishing:** Vishing uses voice communication to deceive a person into disclosure or action.
- **C — Smishing:** Smishing delivers deceptive requests through SMS or similar text-message channels.

Coverage: M09; CEH v5 domain 4; Social-engineering channels.
Technical references: [CISA phishing guidance](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world/recognize-and-report-phishing) · [MITRE phishing](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1566/) · [NIST assessment guidance](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/115/final)

### CEH26-M09-Q002

**Answer: B — Authority**

Authority cues exploit apparent rank or trusted institutional status to discourage verification.

Why the other choices do not identify the asked-for mechanism or decision:

- **A — Urgency:** Urgency pressures a person to act quickly before examining the request.
- **C — Scarcity:** Scarcity suggests that an opportunity or resource is limited and may soon disappear.
- **D — Reciprocity:** Reciprocity exploits a person's perceived obligation to return a favor or benefit.

Coverage: M09; CEH v5 domain 4; Influence mechanisms.
Technical references: [CISA phishing guidance](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world/recognize-and-report-phishing) · [MITRE phishing](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1566/) · [NIST assessment guidance](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/115/final)

### CEH26-M09-Q003

**Answer: A — Tailgating**

Tailgating gains entry by following an authorized person through an access-controlled boundary without proper authorization.

Why the other choices do not identify the asked-for mechanism or decision:

- **B — Baiting:** Baiting offers an appealing object or benefit to induce an unsafe action, such as opening untrusted media.
- **C — Shoulder surfing:** Shoulder surfing observes a person's screen, keyboard or similar visible activity to learn sensitive information.
- **D — Dumpster diving:** Dumpster diving seeks useful information from discarded materials.

Coverage: M09; CEH v5 domain 4; Physical deception.
Technical references: [CISA phishing guidance](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world/recognize-and-report-phishing) · [MITRE phishing](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1566/) · [NIST assessment guidance](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/115/final)

### CEH26-M09-Q004

**Answer: B — Independent callback**

Verify a sensitive request through a known trusted contact path, not a number or link supplied by the requester.

Why the other choices do not identify the asked-for mechanism or decision:

- **A — Dual approval:** Require a second authorized reviewer for sensitive changes or transfers so one deceived person is insufficient.
- **C — Reporting channel:** A simple trusted reporting path lets recipients raise suspicious requests without continuing the interaction.
- **D — Phishing-resistant authentication:** Origin-bound cryptographic authentication resists many credential-phishing flows, while broader social engineering still needs controls.

Coverage: M09; CEH v5 domain 4; Verification controls.
Technical references: [CISA phishing guidance](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world/recognize-and-report-phishing) · [MITRE phishing](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1566/) · [NIST assessment guidance](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/115/final)

### CEH26-M09-Q005

**Answer: C — Explicit campaign approval**

A social-engineering exercise needs authorized scope, targets, methods and escalation before delivery.

Why the other choices do not identify the asked-for mechanism or decision:

- **A — Constructive debrief:** A useful debrief explains cues and reporting actions without humiliating individuals.
- **B — Outcome-based measurement:** Measure the behavior relevant to the learning goal instead of treating one click count as complete competence evidence.
- **D — Participant protection:** Protect people from unnecessary harm and avoid collecting real passwords or unrelated sensitive information.

Coverage: M09; CEH v5 domain 4; Exercise governance.
Technical references: [CISA phishing guidance](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world/recognize-and-report-phishing) · [MITRE phishing](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1566/) · [NIST assessment guidance](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/115/final)

### CEH26-M09-Q006

**Answer: D — Business email compromise**

BEC manipulates business communication to induce actions such as fraudulent payments; malware is not required.

Why the other choices do not identify the asked-for mechanism or decision:

- **A — Synthetic-media impersonation:** Generated voice or video can imitate a trusted person, so apparent likeness alone is insufficient verification.
- **B — QR-code phishing:** A QR code can conceal a destination until scanned; the encoded link still needs independent trust checks.
- **C — MFA fatigue:** Repeated authentication prompts can pressure a person into approving an attacker-initiated login.

Coverage: M09; CEH v5 domain 4; Modern deception distinctions.
